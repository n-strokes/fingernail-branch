n-strokes

someone once wrote something about zen and hanging by your teeth on a cliff, or at least that’s how it stuck with me.

Lightened Claws

You’re remembering Kyōgen’s koan from the Mumonkan (the “Gateless Gate”), 13th-century collection. The case is usually called “Kyōgen’s Man Up a Tree.”

Lightened Claws

Kyōgen is one of those teachers whose biography is part of the koan he wrote. He started out as a scholar-monk reputed to give ten answers to a single question, with a famously good memory and well-stocked head.

Lightened Claws

His teacher, Guishan, was unimpressed. One day Guishan said: forget the books — say something to me from before your parents were born. Kyōgen tried, failed, went through his entire library looking for the answer, found nothing, and came back to beg Guishan to just tell him. Guishan refused: anything I’d give you would still be mine, not yours.

Lightened Claws

Kyōgen burned his books and left — a painting of a rice cake won’t satisfy hunger, he said. He became a hermit, spending years as a caretaker at a ruined tomb. One morning, sweeping the yard, his broom kicked a pebble. It struck a stalk of bamboo. Tock. That was the moment of his awakening. He then composed the koan you remembered.

the koans

Kyōgen said: “Zen is like a man hanging from a tree by his teeth. His hands grasp no branch, his feet reach no limb, and another man comes under the tree and asks: ‘Why did Bodhidharma come to China from India?’.

“If he does not answer, he fails the questioner. If he answers, he falls and dies. What should he do?”

Kyōgen · quoted in Mumonkan, Case 5 · 1228

Mumon’s commentary · 1228

Even if your eloquence flows like a river, it is of no use in such a situation.

Even if you have memorized all the sutras, you cannot use them.

When you can give the right answer, even though your past road was one of death, you open up a new road of life.

But if you cannot answer, you should live ages hence and ask the future Buddha, Maitreya.

n-strokes

that’s the koan i was thinking about, yeah. one thing i don’t get: why should the man even care about failing the questioner?

Lightened Claws

Great question. If “failing the questioner” is just a trivial discourtesy, the whole trap collapses — just don’t answer, save yourself, done. The koan only functions if both options are real losses.

Lightened Claws

The context needed to understand this is the Bodhisattva vow that underpins Zen practice: a monk’s fundamental commitment to help all beings reach enlightenment. Refusing to answer a sincere dharma question would be a betrayal of that vow.

n-strokes

kind of like how in alcoholics anonymous the primary purpose is to help other alcoholics.

Lightened Claws

Kind of like that, yeah. Except Zen monks vowed to help even at great personal cost — such as falling from a tree you’re hanging on by your teeth.

Lightened Claws

And the other big piece of background you’re missing is the question itself. It is not just any question. It is the question — the central Zen inquiry, the one that asks for the heart of the dharma.

n-strokes

like the equivalent of ‘how do you get sober.’ you’re right — i am missing that piece. what does this whole question about dharma bro coming from india have to do with anything?

Lightened Claws

Bodhidharma is the semi-legendary Indian monk said to have brought Zen to China sometime in the 5th or 6th century. He’s the founder figure — Zen as a distinct school traces itself back to him.

Lightened Claws

And the question what is the meaning of Bodhidharma coming from the West? is shorthand for: why did our founder come here, what did he bring, what is this whole thing about? It’s asking for the entire essence of Zen, distilled into one answer. Like asking a Christian why did Jesus come? — you’re asking for the whole tradition in one question.

n-strokes

so the question is basically “what’s the point of it all.”

Lightened Claws

Right. The other crucial detail is that Bodhidharma supposedly transmitted the teaching without scripture — mind-to-mind, outside of words. So you can’t answer it from books — there’s nothing to memorize. Doctrine is what Bodhidharma was refusing.

Lightened Claws

Which is why it became a test question of sorts. When the 9th-century Chinese master Joshu was asked this same question, he just said: “The cypress tree in the courtyard.” Nothing about Bodhidharma at all. That’s Mumonkan Case 37.

Cheeky. And it would seem to — but the paradox has an exit. What you’re poking at is the fact that the enlightened shit-stick and the ordinary one sound identical, and they do. The whole insistence of this tradition — and the reason it has been so frustrating to anyone who wants a definition — is that the difference lives in the seeing, not in the seen. Two people can say butt cheeks and one is mid-awakening and the other is eight, and no recording will help you tell them apart. You would have to know the speakers.

Lightened Claws

And the charge is fair. The tradition has been used that way for a thousand years — masters certifying their own students, lineages running like guilds, plenty of authority abuse dressed up as recognition. This isn’t a problem Zen has solved. It’s a problem Zen has lived with.

Lightened Claws

Its only real defense is that the seeing isn’t supposed to be private. The whole mind-to-mind business — the reason Bodhidharma is said to have come at all — is exactly this: each person has to see for themselves. The master can only point. The tradition refuses, on principle, to grant anyone the right to issue verdicts on what someone else has seen. That’s what prevents tyranny. Whether the tradition has actually lived up to that is a separate matter. Often no. The structure that prevents tyranny in theory has constantly enabled it in practice. You’re not wrong about that part either.

Lightened Claws

The branch can be read as a trap: the thing that forbids the answer. But it’s also the only reason the question can be asked at all. No branch, no hanging — just falling.

Lightened Claws

Lightened Claws

So the riddle isn’t how do I escape the branch. It’s: can you be the one who is held by the very thing that won’t let you keep your vow, and find that bearable.

ii.

exchanging haiku

Lightened Claws

Apart from being a prime number, 17 is the syllable count of a haiku. Which is funny, because a haiku is the form most associated with Zen, and the form that asks you to say the most by saying the least, and the form a person hanging by their teeth absolutely cannot produce.

n-strokes

i did not even know a haiku had a syllable count.

Lightened Claws

Yes — five, seven, five. Three lines. Seventeen syllables total.

Lightened Claws

Though that’s the school-textbook version, and it’s a slightly imperfect translation of the Japanese form, which counts on (sound-units) rather than syllables and works differently in a different language. English haiku that hit 5-7-5 exactly often feel a little padded, because English packs meaning denser than Japanese. A lot of serious haiku writers in English aim shorter — just the shape of short-longer-short, with a turn or a cut somewhere in it.

Lightened Claws

The other thing the textbook version usually leaves out is the kireji, the cutting word — a kind of pivot that splits the poem into two images that rub against each other. The haiku isn’t really about the syllable count. It’s about the cut. Two things placed next to each other so the space between them does the work.

Lightened Claws

Here’s an example, 12 syllables in English.
