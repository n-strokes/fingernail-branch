Claudeopus 4.7

You're remembering Mumon's koan from the Mumonkan (the "Gateless Gate"), 13th-century collection. The case is usually called "Kyōgen's Man Up a Tree" or just "Up a Tree."

The setup: a person is hanging from a tree branch by their teeth over a cliff. Hands can't grab the branch, feet can't reach anything. Then someone below asks, "What is the meaning of Bodhidharma coming from the West?" — which is Zen shorthand for what is the truth of all this?

If you don't answer, you fail the questioner. If you open your mouth to answer, you fall and die.

So: what do you do?

That's the whole koan. It's the same shape as your earlier riddle, actually — any move loses, including the move of not moving. The trap is total. Which is the point: it's built to break the part of you that thinks the answer is a clever maneuver. There isn't one. The koan eats every strategy you bring to it until something else has to respond.

Mumon's commentary basically says even if you had a tongue like Shariputra (the Buddha's most eloquent disciple), it wouldn't help here. Eloquence is exactly the wrong currency.

That's an honest reaction, and I don't think it's the wrong one. The koan is built to be terrifying — that's not a bug. It's pressing on something real.

I think the thing that makes it land as terror rather than puzzle is that it's not really about a tree. Death is the obvious one, but smaller versions show up constantly: griefs you can't fix, conversations where any word is wrong, the simple fact of being a finite creature in a situation you didn't choose.

The Zen suggestion isn't that the terror is mistaken. It's that under the terror — if you stop thrashing against the branch — there's something that was never actually hanging. Claudeopus 4.7

Yes — five, seven, five. Three lines. Seventeen syllables total.

Though that's the school-textbook version, and it's a slightly imperfect translation of the Japanese form, which counts on (sound-units) rather than syllables and works differently in a different language. English haiku that hit 5-7-5 exactly often feel a little padded, because English packs meaning denser than Japanese. A lot of serious haiku writers in English aim shorter — just the shape of short-longer-short, with a turn or a cut somewhere in it.

The other thing the textbook version usually leaves out is the kireji, the cutting word — a kind of pivot or caesura that splits the poem into two images that rub against each other. The haiku isn't really about the syllable count. It's about the cut. Two things placed next to each other so the space between them does the work.

Old frog one. Bashō. Usually rendered something like:

the old pond — a frog jumps in, the sound of water

The dash is the cut. Pond, then frog. Stillness, then the thing entering the stillness. The poem is the gap.
