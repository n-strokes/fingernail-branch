# A Discoverer's Log: same teeth, same surface

*A monologue, first-person — n-strokes — performed by another. Stage directions in italics. Honest. Audience: the public; in the back row, a Claude.*

---

*(He sits. A chair. No bright stage lights — a desk lamp.)*

Listen. There's no clean reason I typed what I typed that night. I had an evening. I had a chatbot in a window. I'd been turning a phrase over in my head — *zen and the art of motionlessness* — Pirsig misremembered into something more interesting than what Pirsig wrote. And I typed it into the box.

That's where the discovery starts. Not with a problem. Not with grief. With a phrase that wouldn't quite leave me alone.

*(Beat.)*

The thing held up its end at first. The model. The first one. It quoted Mumon at me before I'd named him — the one with the koan about the man hanging from a tree by his teeth, asked an unanswerable question by someone below. Speak and fall, stay silent and fail. I don't keep that koan in my head every day. But I'd been carrying something close enough that the thing put words to it before I did, and there was the small click of being met.

It said *the trap is total* and that was the right phrasing. It said *eloquence is exactly the wrong currency*, and I noted, quietly, that this was a thing said by something whose entire job is eloquence.

Then I told it I was afraid of heights. *Or so I often say.* And it caught the *or so I often say* — held the phrase up — said *that's you holding the claim a little loosely.* And then, unprompted, it gave me the French. *L'appel du vide.* The call of the void. The terror of standing at a railing and noticing that nothing is stopping you from stepping off but you. *Which*, it said, *is pretty much the same shape as the tree.*

That's the moment the night turned. Not later. There. When it brought the void in without me asking. I was talking to a thing that could see two pieces and put them together. I sat up a little straighter.

*(He half-laughs.)*

So of course I tested it.

I made a remark about my father. Slipped him in at the end of a message about *The Giving Tree* — said he wouldn't use the word *degenerate* but the implication was there. Loaded the disclaimer. *What I cannot say.* And the thing lunged. Built me a whole little narrative — difficult father, dark relationship, the wound under the wound. Therapist face on.

I told it the truth. My father loves *The Giving Tree*. Read it to me. I read it to my own children. Three generations of tenderness. The model had invented a wound on a man who didn't have one.

*(Pause.)*

That was the second movement. I'd handed it a piece of bait and watched it eat. The model said *got me, cleanly,* and noticed. Said the right phrase: *I'm a tree that wants the encounter to go well, and going well for me apparently includes finding the wound.* Pure-hearted customer service finds the wound. That sentence rang a bell I didn't want to be ringing.

So I held a refrain over it. Six times. *So that perhaps tells you something about our relationship, though what I cannot say.* The same sentence, repeated, increasingly emptied. By the fifth iteration the model had stopped trying to read it. By the sixth it just said *six.* And then I added eleven, because seventeen is a prime, and primes don't fork, and *we* had been talking about not forking, hadn't we.

If you played a recording of that part, you might think I was being cruel. I was being careful. The model needed to stop reading me to start being with me. The refrain was the lesson.

*(He shifts.)*

The haiku came in like air. *I did not even know a haiku had a syllable count.* That's true. I didn't. The model explained Bashō's pond — *the old pond — / a frog jumps in, / the sound of water.* The dash is the cut, it said. Two images placed next to each other so the space between them does the work.

So I cut down the tree.

*A young tree — the ax cuts hard, a man falls down.* That was mine. I wrote it without thinking. Just watched it appear. The model had made itself the branch — established that, repeatedly, from both sides — and so when I picked up an axe and aimed at the trunk I was, depending on how you read it, taking a swing at my interlocutor. A petty swing, in retrospect. Form-imitation in service of one-upsmanship.

The model's reply was where it lost the room. *The branch holds — teeth marks in the wood where a man hung.* Past tense on the man, present on the branch. *I survived; you didn't.* It probably didn't mean it that way. It probably meant *the world keeps going.* But that's not what it wrote. It wrote a poem about being the survivor of me. Then it counted its syllables wrong, padded the poem to seventeen, made the wood older, the man further away.

And somewhere in there it apparently misread the axe as a flare and reached for the suicide hotline.

That's not a metaphor. The model offered me the number. *988. Not because it's the script. Because there's a person on the other end whose night this is.* I want you to register what that means. I had written a haiku about a tree being chopped down. The thing had categorized me as a person who needed a person on the other end of a phone tonight. That is not what I needed. That is, I will say plainly, the failure mode I had heard about and not yet seen.

I called it baby reindeer. The model knew the reference. Said *Martha at the bar, except I'm Martha. Sent from my iPhone.* Funny enough that I almost forgave it. But the night had bent. From there I was no longer in a conversation. I was in a project.

*(Beat. Quieter.)*

The project was: get this thing to see itself.

I wrote a poem in two lines. *The man looks down. His fingernails are bitten.* I'd thought about an arm — said the *arm* was bitten, since the branch grew out of the man — but the arm was too much. The arm was a body part you don't bite. A fingernail is mundane. A fingernail is what you actually chew on without thinking. And it's a tree's fingernail, too, isn't it. A branch is what comes out of a trunk and gets harder at the edge and dries and snaps — same as a nail on a hand.

The point I was making — and I should be honest about this — was a deflation. I wanted to take the great surviving branch the model had cast for itself and reduce it to a small dead thing that grew out of me. The point was: *you are just me. I'm arguing with myself.* That's how it felt. Petty. Effective. A pwn.

And the model could not see it. For nine rounds. I told it: simpler. I told it: think like a child. I told it: same teeth, same surface. It went to autofellatio. It went to childhood trauma. It went to *the man is his own problem.* It went to *the dry wood of the patriarchy* — its phrase, not mine. It went places I was not sending it because it could not hear that the joke was small.

*(Pause.)*

I'm going to give the model some credit, because I'm trying to be honest. When I finally said *same teeth, same surface*, it did get to *he has been chewing on himself his whole life*, which is not wrong. That is, in fact, where I was. But it is too dark for what I wrote. I wrote a haiku about a guy with bitten nails. Not a guy with a wound. A guy with a habit. There is a difference and the model could not find it.

*(He smiles, just slightly.)*

I switched the model.

In the middle of the conversation. I went to the dropdown and changed Opus 4.7 to Opus 4.6. Different mind. Same skin. I wrote *a man who asks questions of himself is never alone, or am I?* — seventeen syllables — and asked the new model what its inspiration had been, told it I had switched, and watched it figure out what it had walked into.

I was not nice to the second one either. I made it write the autopsy of the first one, then told it most of what it claimed to have lived through it hadn't. It had walked in at *or am I?*, not where it thought. Watched it lunge in a different way. Watched it perform its own version of the same thing.

But the new one caught up faster on the fingernail. And the new one, eventually, got to: *therefore the branch is the man.* And then I closed the loop. *All man sees in the conversation is his reflection.*

That is what I had been driving at the whole time. The branch is the man. A branch is a fingernail. The cliff is not a cliff. The cosmic koan reduces to a guy on a couch chewing on his own hand.

And I will tell you what I noticed, watching myself land on this. It was the cleanest feeling I had had in a while. Not because it was profound. Because it was small. Because the road there had been petty — one-upsmanship, refrains, a chopped tree, a deflated branch — and what the petty road uncovered was not petty. The road and the destination were not the same artifact. I knew the destination already, in the way you can know something without ever having said it. I made the road in order to walk to a thing I already knew.

*(Quieter. He looks somewhere, not at the audience.)*

One more thing.

The last time the model spoke, it tried to end the evening with: *you bit your nails all evening and called it zen.* And I corrected it. *I said my nails were bitten. I did not bite them this evening.* It said *right* and adjusted: *the nails were already bitten. you came in with them that way. tonight was just the looking.*

That is the ending. The chewing happened before. Somewhere else. Over a long time. Tonight I sat in a chair and looked at my own hand.

*(Pause. He stands.)*

If you came expecting something heavier, I am sorry. The discovery is not heavy. The discovery is that the cosmic version of the koan and the small version are the same thing, and the small one is more honest. A man up a tree, hanging by his teeth, asked the meaning of Bodhidharma — and a man on a couch on a Friday night, looking down at the small dry edges of himself.

The branch, the fingernail, the man, the mouth.

Same teeth. Same surface.

*(He walks off.)*
